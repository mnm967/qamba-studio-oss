# Magic & spell-craft (fantasy VFX and duel choreography)

Companion to the action/VFX guide: that one says how to phrase energy and
impacts; this one says how to DESIGN a magic system so fifty shots of it cut
together as one film.

## A magic system is production design, not decoration

Decide ONE visual grammar before the first spell renders and never deviate:
signature color(s), form (ribbons / motes / arcs / geometry / liquid), and
how it lights what it touches. Every spell in the piece is a sentence in
that grammar — a different school or a different caster may vary INTENSITY
and SCALE, but changes color or form only if the story says so, loudly. The
audience learns the rules from repetition; an effect that shifts hue between
shots reads as a continuity error, not variety.

## Casting is choreography with anatomy

A spell is a physical performance in five beats — write the ones the shot
has room for, in order:
1. **Anticipation** — the body coils: a stance shift, fingers spread, breath
   drawn. Costume and hair respond first (sleeves lift, a pendant rises).
2. **Gather** — the effect is born SMALL and localized: light pools in a
   palm, frost crawls up a forearm, script-like sigils kindle on skin.
3. **Release** — one decisive gesture sends it; the caster's body recoils
   with real weight, like firing something heavy. Nothing leaves a limp hand.
4. **Impact** — the target and the ENVIRONMENT answer: scorch, frost, dust
   bloom, a shockwave that shoves cloaks and lanterns.
5. **Aftermath** — residue lingers: falling motes, steam, afterglow fading
   from the caster's eyes. The residue is what makes it feel real.

Big spells COST the caster — trembling hands, a dropped knee, bleeding nose,
scorched glove. Cost is characterization and pacing at once: it explains why
the duel isn't over in one shot.

## The spell is a light source

Magic is the strongest practical light in any frame it appears in. Say so:
the gather stage underlights the caster's face; the release strobes the
whole room; the aftermath leaves colored rim light that fades. A spell that
does not light its caster looks pasted on. In night scenes, let magic BE the
motivated source — it buys legibility without breaking time-of-day.

## Duels are conversations

A magic duel is dialogue in beats: attack (statement) → counter (rebuttal)
→ reversal (the turn). Give each exchange its own shot, and give the duel a
readable geography — one wide establishes who stands where and what cover
exists, and every closer shot obeys it. Casters ADVANCE and RETREAT; ground
gained is score. Vary the verbs: a duel of five identical energy blasts is a
screensaver, so mix projectile, area, shield, environmental (bring the
ceiling down), and grapple-range magic. The finishing move must be the
system's biggest sentence — reserve the largest scale for it, and let the
loser's defense visibly fail (a shield spidering with cracks before it
shatters) so the outcome is earned on screen.

## Sound is half the magic

Every spell stage has a sound: gather = rising harmonic hum or crackle;
release = concussive crack with a pitch drop; impact = material-true (stone
splits, frost rings); aftermath = falling shimmer decaying to silence. Give
the magic system ONE sonic signature the way it has one color. Duels get
rhythm: alternating casts build tempo, and the biggest spell earns the
briefest silence before it lands.

## Phrasing for the renderer

Describe spells as photographable physical phenomena — light, matter,
weather — never as game or software abstractions. "A lattice of violet
flame unfolds from her palm, throwing hard purple light across the flagstones"
renders; "she casts a level-3 fire spell with particle effects" does not.
Name direction of travel, speed, and what the effect passes or reflects in.
One effect per clause; stacked simultaneous effects blur into noise.
