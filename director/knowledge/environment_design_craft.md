# Environment design craft — spaces that act

A location is a character with a floor plan. Environments fail on screen in
two ways: as BACKDROPS (a pretty wall nobody can navigate) and as VOIDS
(technically present, visibly uninhabited). Both are design failures, not
rendering failures — they were never written.

## Scale is a decision

State the size of every space in human terms: footprint, ceiling height, how
far the eye can see, how many people it was built for versus how many are in
it. "A vault antechamber three stories tall, built for forty clerks, holding
two intruders" stages itself. Scale contrast is free drama — cut from a
crawlspace to an atrium and the audience feels it before anyone speaks.

## Name the anchor features

Every location gets 2-4 NAMED fixtures — the freight lift, the teller cage,
the glass bridge, the coolant stacks. These are what shots anchor position to
("Mara at the teller cage; Kess on the catwalk above"), what fights break,
and what the audience uses to build a mental map. A location whose features
are never named produces coverage that could be anywhere — and characters who
drift, because "where" was never a writable fact.

## Background life

Decide who and what is in the space by DEFAULT, before the scene's cast
walks in: crowd density, vendors, drones, steam, animals, signage churn,
maintenance workers. State it once per location and keep it consistent —
a market that teems in scene 2 and stands empty in scene 4 (same hour, no
story reason) is a continuity break. Emptiness is a valid design ("the floor
is abandoned, and the emptiness is the point") but must be chosen, stated,
and used. Background figures never interact with the cast — they are
texture, and any one of them the story needs is a character with a name.

## A place makes its own light and sound

Name the light the location generates: neon signage, a wall of monitors,
skylights, fire, emergency strips. Motivated light is what makes a space
photographable from every angle without inventing new sources per shot —
and in a heist, light IS geography (the guard's flashlight cone, the sensor
grid's red wash). Same for sound: a place has a voice before anyone speaks
(vent hum, rain on skylights, market roar, server whine). The soundscape of
a scene starts from the location's, not from silence.

## Multi-room jobs (heists, infiltrations, chases)

When a story moves through sections of one building, design the ROUTE as a
sequence of contrasting rooms — each with its own scale, palette accent,
light and hazard — and state the connective tissue (the duct between them,
the stair, the freight lift). The audience should always know which room
we're in from a single frame, and what direction "deeper" is. One room per
function: the approach, the choke point, the prize, the escape. If two rooms
would read identically in a wide shot, merge them or redesign one.

## Angles agree

An establishing wide, its reverse angle, a detail plate and an atmosphere
plate are four photographs of ONE room: same architecture, same materials,
same light logic. Any plate that contradicts the master is a different
location wearing the name. When a location recurs across scenes at different
hours, the architecture holds and only the light and population change —
say what changed, and only that.
