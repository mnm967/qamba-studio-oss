# Comedy craft — how a sitcom episode is built

This guide is about STRUCTURE and JOKE MECHANICS. It is loaded for comedy
briefs. The camera side of comedy (set up in a wide, pay off in a cut,
reactions get their own shot) is in `directing_craft.md` and
`camera_grammar.md`; this is what has to be true of the writing before a
camera can help it.

## The shape of an episode

A sitcom episode is not a short film with jokes in it. It has a fixed,
recognisable skeleton, and audiences feel the skeleton even when they cannot
name it:

- **COLD OPEN** — before anything is set up. A self-contained bit that is
  funny with zero context and ends on a hard laugh. It establishes the world's
  comic rules, not its plot.
- **A-STORY** — the main character wants something small and concrete, and
  pursues it past the point of reason. Small is the point: a sitcom want is
  "get the good parking space", not "save the restaurant".
- **B-STORY** — a second, lighter want, usually two other characters, running
  in parallel. It exists so the episode can cut away at the moment the A-story
  is most uncomfortable, and so the two can collide at the end.
- **ESCALATION** — each scene raises the stakes of the same small want. The
  character does not learn; they double down. Comedy comes from someone
  refusing to take the easy exit that is visibly available.
- **THE WORST THING** — the point where the lie collapses / everyone is in
  the same room / the thing is discovered. This is the biggest laugh in the
  episode and it should be a consequence of choices the audience watched being
  made, not an accident.
- **RESOLUTION** — the want is resolved, usually by the character getting it
  and finding it worthless, or not getting it and pretending they never wanted
  it. Nobody grows very much; the status quo restores.
- **TAG** — a short scene after the resolution, often the B-story's punchline
  or a callback to the cold open. It is the last thing the audience hears, so
  it is a joke and nothing else.

**Status is the engine.** Most sitcom scenes are about who is above whom, and
the funniest moments are status reversals: the confident one humiliated, the
overlooked one briefly in charge. Know, in every scene, who has status at the
start and who has it at the end.

## How a scene works

- A comedy scene enters LATE and leaves EARLY. Start after the premise is
  already established and cut on the laugh — never let a scene explain
  itself first or wind down afterwards.
- Every scene ends on a **button**: its hardest line. If a scene trails off
  into action, its ending has been thrown away.
- Scenes are dialogue-DENSE. Talk is the medium. A comedy scene at a drama's
  pace is a drama.
- Somebody in the scene is the **straight man**. The flat, literal reply to an
  absurd statement is usually the biggest laugh in an exchange, and a scene
  where everyone is a comedian is a scene with nothing to push against.

## How a joke works

- **The funny word goes last.** In the line and in the exchange. Rewrite for
  end-position; a joke with the reveal in the middle is a joke defused.
- **Never explain after the laugh.** If a line lands and the next line
  comments on it, cut the comment.
- **Specific is funny; general is not.** Name the brand, the number, the day
  of the week. "A sandwich" is nothing. "The egg salad sandwich he has been
  describing since Tuesday" is a joke.
- **The third one is the twist.** Where there is a list or a repetition, make
  it three and break the pattern on the third.
- **The joke must be THIS person's.** A line that any character could have
  said is a joke belonging to the writer, not the cast. Comedy is character:
  the same information delivered through a specific person's damage.
- **Confidence, never winking.** Characters do not signal that they are
  joking, do not say "I'm kidding", do not laugh at their own lines. They
  mean it completely — that is what makes it funny.
- **Runners pay off.** Plant a distinctive phrase or object early and bring it
  back later, changed by what has happened in between. The callback is what
  makes an episode feel written rather than assembled, and the tag is where
  the best one lands.

## What kills it

- Everyone being witty in the same voice.
- Jokes about how funny the situation is.
- Characters who take the sensible option.
- Emotional honesty arriving early — a sitcom character admitting the truth in
  act one has ended the episode.
- Explaining a callback ("like I said earlier…"). Say the phrase; the audience
  remembers.
