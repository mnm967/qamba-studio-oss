# Directing craft — scene grammar

## Coverage patterns

Classic scene shape: orient (establishing wide or telling insert) → develop
(mediums, two-shots as characters engage) → intensify (closer as stakes rise)
→ punctuate (the scene's final image is its thesis). Break the shape on
purpose, not by accident: opening on an extreme close-up creates mystery that
a later wide pays off. Screen direction stays consistent within a scene —
if she exits frame right, she enters the next shot frame left. Keep an
imaginary axis between two facing characters and shoot from one side of it;
crossing it reads as disorientation (use deliberately in fights and reveals).

## Cutting rhythm

Cut on motion — a turn, a reach, a door swing — and the edit disappears. Hold
static compositions through emotional weight; cut fast through mechanical
process. Every cut should change at least two of: shot size, angle, subject.
A reaction is usually worth more than the action twice: show the strike once,
then the face that felt it.

## Fight choreography

Fights are geography plus cause-and-effect. Establish the arena and both
fighters' positions in a wide before the first exchange; return to a wide
whenever positions change meaningfully. Each exchange: intention (weight
shift, guard up) → strike → impact (the frame that sells force) → consequence
(stumble, blood, broken prop). Alternate wide geography shots with close
impacts. Vary weapons-of-opportunity and surfaces — environment participating
is what makes screen fights memorable. Speed contrast sells power: a held
breath, then the burst. Never let both fighters blur into motion soup; one
clear action at a time.

## Color and light

Choose a palette of 3–4 named colors per scene and motivate every light
source: window, practical lamp, neon sign, fire, screen glow. Contrast
temperature to separate subject from world (warm face against cool street).
Emotional grammar: desaturate for numbness, push saturation at emotional
peaks, shift hue across an arc (the same room colder after the loss). Night
is never black — it's blue, sodium orange, neon magenta.

## Costume and production design

Clothes are biography: wear, fit, and one signature piece per character that
survives every scene. Environments need history — clutter that implies use,
repair, habit. A prop introduced deliberately (held, touched, framed in
insert) is a promise to the audience; pay it off.

## One-takes

A one-take earns its length by continuous revelation: the camera keeps
finding new information (a new room, a new character, a reversal) without
cutting. Write them as a single beat with a clear path — who the camera
follows, what it passes, where it lands. Best at 8–12 seconds in this
pipeline; the landing composition must be strong because it chains onward.
